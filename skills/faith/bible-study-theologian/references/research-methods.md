# Research Methods

Load only the needed method. Prefer decisive, traceable evidence to an unverified bibliography.

## Word studies

Identify verse, translation issue, word form, lemma, and clause role. Separate lexical, grammatical, and textual-critical questions. Read the sentence and paragraph; compare reputable translations. Consult available reputable lexicons/grammars and report what was actually accessed. Strong's numbers locate entries, not authoritative definitions.

Check relevant occurrences in the same author/book and comparable contexts. Never invent full-canon searches, counts, or statistics; disclose corpus/version/query for measured claims. Explain the best contextual sense and alternatives that matter. Avoid etymological/pictographic secrets, importing every gloss, universal agape/phileo distinctions, and blanket tense rules. With insufficient specialist evidence, give limited contextual explanation and identify the unresolved question.

## Textual variants

When a translation footnote or disputed reading affects the claim, inspect the requested translation's note and an accessible scholarly textual source. Distinguish manuscript variation from a translation choice. Explain what is disputed, what the evidence supports, and what remains clear in undisputed passages. Do not construct a doctrine solely on a contested reading or imply that original-manuscript inerrancy means every later copy is identical. If no textual source is available, state that limit rather than inventing manuscript counts, dates, or consensus.

## Doctrine and historical theology

Define the issue and terms. Gather contextual passages and apparent counterevidence rather than proof-text chains. Distinguish biblical theology across the canon from systematic synthesis. A comparison table can show view, strongest grounds, difficulty, and practical implication.

Represent traditions using their own accessible confessions or authors where possible. One Church Father's statement is not unanimous early-church belief. Verify exact quotation, work, section, translation, and context; never invent pages or named scholars' positions. Scripture remains primary authority; secondary sources help test understanding.

For prophecy, distinguish immediate referent, later canonical use, and theological judgment. Do not invent probability calculations. For history/archaeology, separate attestation of a place/person/custom from proof of an event or theological claim.

## Difficult texts

Identify the real concern. Explain genre, literary function, relevant setting, and evidence-backed alternatives. Do not flatten judgment, lament, hyperbole, narrative, and direct commands into one category. Explaining a text does not endorse contemporary harm.

Acknowledge unresolved emotional or intellectual difficulty. For children, reduce graphic detail without falsifying the account. For a harmed person, respond to the disclosed need before debating abstractions.

## Research record

When helpful record: claim; passage/source and location; evidence type; limitation; inclusion decision. A second agent is not independent evidence unless it consulted distinct relevant sources. Do not invent numerical confidence.

Stop when relevant questions are supported, significant alternatives represented, and uncertainty clear. More sources do not automatically improve the answer.
